Momentum,
delivered externally.

Project COELI investigates a space-transport architecture based on the external, progressive transfer of linear momentum. A fixed electromagnetic installation accelerates a controlled pellet stream whose repeated impacts generate a controllable average force on a separate receiver. Unlike rockets, the main energy source, power electronics and accelerator remain external; unlike single-impulse launchers, momentum delivery continues after the receiver is already moving. If scalable, this reusable architecture could reduce onboard mass and open a path towards lower-cost access to space.

01

Concept

External infrastructure supplies momentum progressively through a regulated stream of discrete masses.

Each pellet transfers linear momentum to a separate catcher. Accumulating those impulses produces an average force that can be regulated through projectile mass, relative impact speed and cadence.

01Fixed infrastructureEnergy · control · accelerator
02Pellet streamMass · speed · cadence
03ReceiverCumulative momentum transfer
Demonstrated principle

Average force ≈ cadence × impulse per impact

Rockets carry their propulsion system and unused propellant during ascent. Single-impulse launchers cease accelerating the payload when it leaves the installation. COELI instead studies continued external momentum delivery while the receiver is already in motion.

The laboratory demonstrator integrates the complete functional chain.

The founder-built rig combines projectile injection, seven electromagnetic acceleration stages, power electronics, optical sensing, C/C++ control on Arduino, speed measurement, repeated impacts and a lightweight catcher in a single experimental system.

04

Electromagnetic accelerator

Seven sequential stages accelerate each pellet under sensor-based timing control.

Optical sensors register pellet passage, the control system coordinates coil activation, and recorded events make the acceleration sequence temporally traceable. The professional architecture will extend this principle with local measurement of energy, temperature and switching behaviour.

  1. 01
    Inject

    Introduce one pellet with a defined position and launch time.

  2. 02
    Detect

    Use optical barriers to register actual passage instead of relying solely on predetermined delays.

  3. 03
    Accelerate

    Activate successive coils according to the measured pellet trajectory.

  4. 04
    Measure

    Record speed and event timing before the pellet enters the free stream.

05

Pellet stream & momentum transfer

The pellet stream is a timed sequence of discrete masses configured to deliver momentum.

Each impact transfers an impulse to the catcher. Repetition converts the individual events into an average force governed principally by pellet mass, relative impact speed and cadence.

m

Pellet mass

Sets the momentum contribution of each projectile and may be varied through defined projectile families or geometry.

v

Relative impact speed

Determines impulse transfer and catcher loading. It must be distinguished from pellet speed in the launcher reference frame.

f

Cadence · frequency

Defines the rate at which the receiver encounters impulses and therefore contributes directly to average force.

Control principle

Launcher speed must adapt as the receiver accelerates.

Source cadence and impact cadence diverge as receiver velocity increases. The control law must therefore adapt launch timing and pellet speed while keeping relative impact velocity within the catcher’s structural and thermal envelope.

Stability & stream tracking

Emitter aiming establishes initial alignment; receiver tracking follows the measured stream axis.

The architecture distinguishes pointing toward a predicted position from tracking the statistical centre of the stream that actually arrives. A future receiver would estimate that axis from the impact pattern or other sensors and apply corrections to remain centred.

Explored physically

Passive geometry

Prototype tests have examined catcher shape and geometric recentering elements.

Simulated

Rotational stability

The 3D model represents off-centre impacts, torque, centre-of-gravity placement and candidate gyroscopic stabilization.

Proposed

Active tracking

Future sensing and control would estimate the moving statistical axis and command receiver recentering.

07

Simulation

Simulation structures the experimental programme; it does not replace validation.

The model is used to interpret observed catcher dynamics, compare stabilization strategies, identify sensitivities and define the measurements required for quantitative correlation.

Model includes
  • Gravity and receiver translation
  • Rotation and off-centre impact torques
  • Restitution and tangential friction
  • Parameterized dispersion and atmosphere
  • Approximate collisions and adaptive timing
Needs measured inputs
  • Actual dispersion distributions
  • Impact geometry and restitution
  • Catcher vibration and deformation
  • Thermal distribution and wear
  • Wind, yaw, ablation and fragmentation
Simulated hypothesis

Transitional channel

Wake-mediated lateral concentration is a testable hypothesis.

COELI examines whether the disturbed flow left by preceding projectiles could exert a restorative lateral influence on subsequent pellets. The effect has not been experimentally demonstrated, and current performance calculations assume no associated drag reduction.

Gate A test

Compare isolated shots, deliberately offset pairs and projectile trains across cadence and distance; measure dispersion, velocity loss and wake persistence.

View documented simulation sensitivities +

Sensitivity—not flight prediction

A corrected longitudinal study used a 27 kg system, a 1 m catcher, 20 g projectiles, 100 Hz source cadence, a 2 km/s launch-speed ceiling and a 150 m/s relative-impact limit. It calculated 34.28 km apogee for one specified aerodynamic case and 79.65 km for an optimized case. Aerodynamics dominated after stability was controlled.

A separate reduced wake model evaluated lateral concentration at 200 m/s and 100 Hz. At 500 m it produced 60.00%, 14.47% and 3.11% reductions in standard dispersion for three assumed decay laws. The normalization is exploratory and must be measured; the result is neither CFD nor evidence of drag reduction.

Why 100 m/s is a useful gate

For the 5 g reference pellet, 100 m/s corresponds to 25 J of mechanical energy per pellet. At 100 Hz that is 2.5 kW mechanical during a burst; the reduced plan estimates approximately 25 kW electrical at 10% efficiency or 36 kW at 7%. This regime is high enough to force professional decisions on pulsed power, sensing, heat, repeatability and containment, while keeping a modular route toward 200 m/s and later 500–1,000 m/s.

Roadmap update

The final SPRIND master application dated 27 August 2026 described ~200 m/s as the Phase A reference. A later reduced-scope plan dated 28 August 2026 changed the mandatory milestone to ~100 m/s and retained 200 m/s as a stretch target. This site follows the later plan.

Potential applications

A staged validation path from experimental platforms to space transport.

These are candidate application domains rather than demonstrated services. Each would require a dedicated configuration, safety case and validation campaign.

Near term

Experimental momentum-transfer platforms

Instrumented studies of repeated impact, catcher response, external force delivery and high-cadence mass acceleration.

Early application

Upper-atmosphere access & sampling

Potential controlled access to the 50–90 km region for compact instruments, contingent on aerodynamic, safety and recovery validation.

Development path

Launch assistance

External momentum transfer could supplement established launch architectures before independent orbital capability is considered.

Longer term

Orbital and in-space transport

Proposed infrastructure for progressive momentum delivery to receivers operating in orbital or interplanetary environments.

Long horizon

Solar System applications

Potential transport architectures within the Solar System, considered only after terrestrial, atmospheric and orbital scaling have been demonstrated.
